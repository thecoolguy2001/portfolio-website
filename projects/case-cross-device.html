Case Study 02

Simplifying Cross-Device Experiences

Deciding what belongs on a watch, a handheld, a phone or a desktop — what each surface is uniquely good at, and what should never ask for attention at all.

The question

When the same product runs on a watch, a handheld, a phone and a desktop, what actually belongs on each one — and what should never ask for attention at all?

The position

Surfaces do not differ by screen size, they differ by how long someone will look. Design to the available attention span first; the layout follows from that.

Where it stands

Seven shipped products across four device classes: three Apple Watch apps, three Flipper Zero apps, and one product (Visualizer) that runs on web, iPhone and Mac.

The question

Screen size is the wrong variable

The usual way to describe cross-device work is responsive: one design, reflowed to fit. That works for documents. It falls apart the moment the devices are genuinely different in kind, because the thing that changes between a watch and a desktop is not how many pixels are available. It is how long the user is willing to look.

A glance at a watch is two seconds, often one-handed, often mid-activity. A Flipper Zero is picked up deliberately, held, and read at desk distance on a monochrome display. A phone gets minutes and full touch. A Mac gets a session, a keyboard, a trackpad and possibly a second monitor.

Designing from attention rather than dimensions changes what the question even is. It stops being “how do I fit this here” and becomes “what is the single thing this surface is better at than the others, and what should it refuse to do?”

Apple Watch

Two seconds, one hand, and a screen too small to be the interface

Three shipped watchOS apps, all built around the same constraint: the display is too small to be the primary channel, so something else has to be.

Reflex-o is a reflex game, which on a watch sounds like a bad idea — tiny targets, unreliable audio, weak feedback. The resolution was to stop treating the screen as the interface. Commands arrive as voice prompts; results arrive as haptics with distinct signatures for right and wrong. The wrist reports the outcome before the eyes catch up. The score sits tiny, top-of-screen and low-contrast so it is visible mid-game without competing with the command word.

Moodbeat takes the opposite route and makes the screen do exactly one thing. Heart rate and HRV map to a colour ring that fills nearly the whole display, with supporting elements pushed to the edges. No labels, no charts. A chart would be the obvious choice for biometric data and would be unreadable in the two seconds a watch actually gets.

Fibber structures a heart-rate “lie detector” as three phases in under a minute, with a haptic marking each transition. The haptic is doing timing work the screen cannot do, because the user is looking at the person they are questioning, not at their wrist.

Flipper Zero

A handheld that is not a phone

Three shipped Flipper Zero apps, on a small monochrome display with a directional pad and no touch. The temptation is to treat it as a worse phone. It is more useful to treat it as a different class of object: picked up on purpose, read at desk distance, and expected to work without a network.

Pack Track is a shipment tracker that reads a plain text file from the SD card, offline, up to twelve packages. The most important line on screen is inverted for contrast so it is readable at arm’s length. Live tracking exists but is optional and provider-agnostic — the user supplies the URL, key and JSON fields rather than being locked to a service. Notably, it never notifies. Checking a package is not worth an interruption, so the product does not generate one.

Motion Lab turns the Video Game Module into a measuring instrument: peak g-force, rotation rate, swing tempo, plus a 100 Hz CSV logger. The design question was what a handheld with no keyboard is good for, and the answer was capture-now-analyse-later. The device records cleanly and hands the data off; it does not try to be the analysis environment.

Pod uses sub-GHz radio for Flipper-to-Flipper encounters, logging who you crossed paths with and settling it in a ten-second tap race, with no add-on hardware. It is the one case where the device’s most interesting capability is one a phone cannot easily replicate at all.

One product, three surfaces

Visualizer on web, iPhone and Mac

Visualizer began as a web app and ran that way for ten months before native iOS and macOS ports were built in Swift. That sequencing matters: the shared layer was already proven before it had to be shared.

What is held constant across surfaces is the effect library, the colour system and the motion language — so the product reads as one tool. What changes is everything about how it is driven.

One identity, three layouts

iPhone, Mac and web each get their own layout rather than a reflow of a single design. Shared catalogue, shared colour, shared motion; separate structure.

Input follows the surface

Touch-first on iPhone: pinch, swipe, long-press. Trackpad and keyboard on Mac with hover states and full keyboard control. The web build keeps the keyboard as primary.

The canvas stays chromeless

No on-screen buttons on any surface. Controls live on the keyboard, or in gestures, so the screen stays the thing you are actually looking at.

Optimisations live in the port

The Swift builds re-implement the audio path natively but share the effect catalogue one-to-one. Surface-specific performance work stays in the port rather than contaminating the shared effects.

The Mac build is designed to be comfortable on a second monitor, and the iOS build respects Low Power Mode and the system Reduce Motion setting. Both are cases of the surface, not the product, deciding how much it is allowed to demand.

The rule

What each surface is for

Across the seven products, the division that holds up is roughly this.

Watch — one fact, non-visually

A single piece of state, delivered through haptics or colour so it does not require a sustained look. If it needs a legend, it does not belong here.

Handheld — capture and glance

Deliberate pickup, offline-first, high contrast, readable at desk distance. Good at recording and at showing a short list. Not an analysis surface.

Phone — the full interaction

Touch gestures, longer sessions, the place where the product can be explored rather than checked.

Desktop — depth and control

Keyboard, precision input, multi-window, background operation. The only surface where it is reasonable to expose the full control set.

Attention

What should never demand attention

The sharper half of the question is not what each surface shows — it is what none of them should interrupt for. Three decisions across these products answer it the same way.

Pack Track never sends a notification, even though shipment status is exactly the kind of data products usually push. LE sits idle and silent until addressed, animating only with intent. Visualizer’s canvas has no persistent chrome, so nothing on screen is competing with the thing the user came to look at.

The common thread is that each product had an obvious reason to interrupt and chose not to. Deciding what a product will not do is harder to show in a portfolio than a feature list, but on small and ambient surfaces it is most of the design work.

Status

Shipped versus concept

Everything in this case study is built and running, with one exception noted below.

Shipped

Reflex-o, Fibber, Moodbeat — Apple Watch

All three on the App Store. Built May to June 2025, solo.

Shipped

Pack Track, Motion Lab, Pod — Flipper Zero

All three complete; Pack Track open-sourced on GitHub.

Shipped

Visualizer — web

Live and in use at visualizerwebsite.com since 2024, with ongoing releases.

In development

Visualizer — iOS and macOS ports

Swift ports sharing the effect catalogue with the web build. Layouts and input models are designed; the ports are not yet released.
